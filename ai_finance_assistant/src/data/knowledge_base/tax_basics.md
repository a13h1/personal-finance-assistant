# Tax Basics for Investors

Understanding how taxes affect your investments is essential to maximizing long-term wealth. Even small differences in tax treatment can result in tens of thousands of dollars over a lifetime of investing. This guide covers the fundamental tax concepts every investor should know.

## Federal Income Tax Brackets (2024)

The U.S. uses a progressive tax system. You don't pay the same rate on all income—only on income within each bracket.

**Single Filer Brackets:**
- 10%: $0 - $11,600
- 12%: $11,601 - $47,150
- 22%: $47,151 - $100,525
- 24%: $100,526 - $191,950
- 32%: $191,951 - $243,725
- 35%: $243,726 - $609,350
- 37%: Over $609,350

**Married Filing Jointly:** Brackets are roughly double the single filer amounts.

Your **marginal tax rate** is the rate on your last dollar of income. Your **effective tax rate** is the average rate paid on all income—always lower than the marginal rate.

## Ordinary Income vs. Capital Gains

Investment income is taxed differently depending on its type:

**Ordinary income** (wages, interest, short-term capital gains) is taxed at regular income tax rates.

**Long-term capital gains** (assets held over 1 year) are taxed at preferential rates:
- 0%: For those in the 10-12% income brackets
- 15%: For most middle and upper-middle income taxpayers
- 20%: For high income earners (over ~$518,900 for singles)

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** An additional 3.8% Medicare surtax applies to investment income for high earners (over $200,000 single, $250,000 married).

## Tax-Efficient Investing Strategies

**Asset Location:**
Different assets belong in different account types:
- **Tax-advantaged accounts (IRA, 401k):** Hold bonds, REITs, high-dividend stocks—assets that generate ordinary income
- **Taxable accounts:** Hold stocks you plan to hold long-term, index funds with low turnover, tax-managed funds

**Buy and Hold:**
The longer you hold an investment without selling, the longer you defer capital gains taxes. Unrealized gains are not taxed.

**Tax-Loss Harvesting:**
Selling investments at a loss to offset capital gains from other investments. Rules:
- Can offset any amount of capital gains with losses
- If losses exceed gains, can deduct up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year
- Excess losses carry forward to future years
- **Wash sale rule:** Cannot repurchase the same or substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale

**Qualified Dividends:**
Dividends from U.S. corporations and certain foreign companies held for the required holding period are taxed at lower long-term capital gains rates.

## State Taxes

Most states also tax investment income. State rates range from 0% (no state income tax: Florida, Texas, Nevada, Washington, etc.) to over 13% (California). This significantly affects after-tax returns, especially for high earners.

## Estimated Taxes

If you have significant investment income not subject to withholding (capital gains from selling investments, large dividends), you may need to pay quarterly estimated taxes to avoid underpayment penalties.

## Key Tax Forms

- **1099-DIV:** Reports dividends received
- **1099-B:** Reports proceeds from security sales (used to calculate capital gains/losses)
- **1099-INT:** Reports interest income
- **Schedule D:** Reports capital gains and losses on your tax return
- **8949:** Itemizes individual security sale transactions

## Common Tax Mistakes

1. **Ignoring the wash sale rule** after tax-loss harvesting
2. **Forgetting cost basis** for inherited securities (receive stepped-up basis)
3. **Overlooking state taxes** when calculating after-tax returns
4. **Not adjusting cost basis** for reinvested dividends
5. **Missing estimated tax payments** on large capital gains events

*This is educational content, not financial advice. Tax laws change frequently—consult a tax professional.*
