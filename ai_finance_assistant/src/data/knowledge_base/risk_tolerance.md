# Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is your ability and willingness to endure declines in the value of your investments in exchange for the possibility of higher returns. Understanding your risk tolerance is one of the most important steps in building an investment strategy that you can stick with through market volatility.

## Two Dimensions of Risk Tolerance

**Ability to Take Risk (Financial Capacity):**
This is the objective side—determined by your financial situation:
- Time horizon: The longer you have before needing the money, the more risk you can afford
- Income stability: A secure job with predictable income allows more investment risk
- Existing assets: An emergency fund and other savings provide a buffer
- Debt obligations: High debt payments limit your financial flexibility

**Willingness to Take Risk (Psychological Tolerance):**
This is the subjective side—determined by your personality:
- How would you feel if your portfolio dropped 30% in 6 months?
- Have you invested before? Have you experienced a market crash?
- How quickly do you make financial decisions?

Both dimensions matter. Someone with a 30-year horizon but extreme anxiety about market declines may be better off with a more conservative portfolio—one they'll actually stick with.

## Risk Tolerance Categories

**Conservative:**
- Priority: Preserving capital over growth
- Typical portfolio: 20-40% stocks, 60-80% bonds and cash
- Expected volatility: Low
- Suitable for: Retirees, those with short time horizons, or highly risk-averse investors

**Moderate:**
- Priority: Balanced growth and stability
- Typical portfolio: 50-70% stocks, 30-50% bonds
- Expected volatility: Medium
- Suitable for: Mid-career investors with medium time horizons

**Aggressive:**
- Priority: Maximum long-term growth
- Typical portfolio: 80-100% stocks, 0-20% bonds
- Expected volatility: High
- Suitable for: Young investors with long time horizons and high psychological tolerance

## The 100-Minus-Age Rule

A traditional rule of thumb suggests subtracting your age from 100 to determine your stock allocation. A 30-year-old would hold 70% stocks; a 60-year-old would hold 40% stocks.

However, with people living longer and low interest rates on bonds, many financial planners now use 110 or 120 minus age as a more modern guideline.

## Sequence of Returns Risk

Near retirement, the order of returns matters significantly. A large market decline in the first years of retirement forces you to sell more shares to meet living expenses, permanently reducing your portfolio's recovery potential. This "sequence of returns risk" means investors approaching retirement should typically reduce stock exposure even if they psychologically could handle volatility.

## Reassessing Your Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is not static. It changes with:
- Life events (marriage, children, job loss)
- Market experience (surviving a 40% crash changes perspective)
- Time horizon changes (getting closer to retirement)
- Wealth accumulation (having more cushion can increase tolerance)

Revisit your risk tolerance assessment at least every 3-5 years or after major life changes.

## Common Risk Tolerance Mistakes

1. **Overestimating tolerance in bull markets:** Many investors believe they can handle losses until they experience them
2. **Panic selling:** The most damaging behavior—locking in losses and missing the recovery
3. **Ignoring time horizon:** A 25-year-old being too conservative sacrifices decades of compound growth
4. **Not considering income stability:** Freelancers and self-employed people should be more conservative given income uncertainty
