# Portfolio Rebalancing

Portfolio rebalancing is the process of realigning the weightings of a portfolio's assets to maintain your original desired level of asset allocation and risk. Over time, as different assets grow at different rates, your portfolio drifts from its target allocation, changing your risk exposure without any deliberate decision on your part.

## Why Rebalancing Is Necessary

Consider a portfolio with a target allocation of 60% stocks and 40% bonds. After a strong bull market, stocks might grow to represent 75% of the portfolio. Now you have more risk than you originally intended—and if a market correction occurs, the loss will be larger than planned.

Rebalancing restores the intended risk/return profile by selling assets that have grown above their target weight and buying assets that have fallen below.

## Rebalancing Methods

**Calendar Rebalancing:**
Rebalance at fixed intervals (quarterly, semi-annually, or annually) regardless of portfolio drift. Simple to implement and prevents excessive trading.

**Threshold Rebalancing (Percentage-of-Portfolio):**
Rebalance when an asset class drifts more than a set percentage from its target (commonly 5%). For example, if stocks are targeted at 60%, you rebalance if they exceed 65% or fall below 55%. More precise but requires monitoring.

**Hybrid Approach:**
Check the portfolio periodically (quarterly) but only rebalance if drift exceeds a threshold. This is generally the recommended approach for most investors.

## The Rebalancing Process

1. **Review current allocations:** Calculate the current percentage of each asset class
2. **Compare to targets:** Identify which assets are over or under-weighted
3. **Determine trades needed:** Calculate how much to sell/buy to restore balance
4. **Execute trades:** Sell overweight assets, buy underweight assets

**Example:**
- Target: 70% stocks ($70,000), 30% bonds ($30,000) on $100,000 portfolio
- After 1 year: Stocks grew to $84,000 (76%), bonds flat at $28,000 (25%), total $112,000
- To rebalance to 70/30: Need 70% × $112,000 = $78,400 in stocks; sell $5,600 of stocks, buy bonds

## Rebalancing in Tax-Advantaged Accounts

The most tax-efficient rebalancing happens inside 401(k) and IRA accounts, where trades don't trigger capital gains taxes. Prioritize rebalancing there before touching taxable accounts.

In taxable accounts:
- Selling appreciated assets triggers capital gains taxes
- Prefer directing new contributions to underweighted assets ("rebalancing with new money")
- If selling, prioritize tax-loss harvesting opportunities

## Does Rebalancing Improve Returns?

Rebalancing doesn't consistently improve returns—it's primarily a risk management tool. Some research shows slight return improvements from "buy low, sell high" dynamic, but results vary.

The real benefit: rebalancing keeps your risk level consistent with your plan and prevents portfolio drift from inadvertently exposing you to more risk than intended.

## Rebalancing Frequency

- **Too frequent (monthly):** Excessive trading costs and taxes erode returns
- **Annually:** Standard approach; balances simplicity and discipline
- **Too infrequent (never):** Portfolio drifts substantially from intended risk level

Studies suggest annual or semi-annual rebalancing with a 5% drift threshold is optimal for most investors.

## Practical Tips

- Automate when possible: Many 401(k) plans offer automatic rebalancing
- Use new contributions: Direct new investments to underweighted asset classes
- Consider tax implications in taxable accounts before selling
- Stay disciplined: Rebalancing forces you to sell high and buy low, which is emotionally difficult
- Don't over-optimize: The benefits of frequent rebalancing are marginal; simplicity wins
