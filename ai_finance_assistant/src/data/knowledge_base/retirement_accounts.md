# Retirement Accounts: 401(k) and IRA

Retirement accounts are special investment accounts created by the government that offer significant tax advantages to encourage long-term saving. The two most common types are the 401(k) and the Individual Retirement Account (IRA).

## 401(k) Plans

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan. Contributions are made with pre-tax dollars, reducing your taxable income in the year of contribution.

### Key 401(k) Features (2024)

- **Contribution limit:** $23,000 per year ($30,500 if age 50+)
- **Employer matching:** Many employers match contributions up to a certain percentage. This is free money—always contribute at least enough to capture the full match
- **Investment options:** Limited to the menu provided by your employer's plan
- **Withdrawals:** Taxed as ordinary income; 10% penalty if withdrawn before age 59½
- **Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs):** Must begin at age 73

### The Value of Employer Match

If your employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary, and your salary is $80,000:
- You contribute $4,800 (6% of salary)
- Employer adds $2,400 (50% match)
- Effective return on your contribution: 50% instantly

Never leave employer matching money on the table—it's the highest guaranteed return you'll ever get.

## Traditional IRA

An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is opened independently through a brokerage, not through an employer. Traditional IRA contributions may be tax-deductible depending on your income and whether you have a workplace retirement plan.

### Key Traditional IRA Features (2024)

- **Contribution limit:** $7,000 per year ($8,000 if age 50+)
- **Tax deductibility:** Full deduction if no workplace plan; phase-out ranges apply if you have a 401(k)
- **Investment flexibility:** Can invest in almost any stock, bond, ETF, or mutual fund
- **Withdrawals:** Taxed as ordinary income; 10% penalty before age 59½
- **RMDs:** Required beginning at age 73

## Roth IRA

The Roth IRA is funded with after-tax dollars, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. This makes it especially valuable for younger investors in lower tax brackets today who expect to be in higher brackets in retirement.

### Key Roth IRA Features (2024)

- **Contribution limit:** Same as Traditional IRA ($7,000/$8,000)
- **Income limits:** Phase out at $146,000-$161,000 for single filers; $230,000-$240,000 for married filing jointly
- **Tax treatment:** No deduction now; tax-free growth and withdrawals
- **No RMDs:** You are never forced to take distributions during your lifetime
- **Roth IRA rule:** Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn any time without penalty

## Roth vs. Traditional: Which Is Better?

The answer depends on whether you expect your tax rate to be higher now or in retirement:

- **Lower taxes now than in retirement → Roth IRA** (pay taxes now at lower rate; withdraw tax-free later)
- **Higher taxes now than in retirement → Traditional IRA/401(k)** (deduct now at high rate; pay taxes later at lower rate)

Young, lower-income earners almost always benefit from Roth accounts. High-income earners in peak earning years typically benefit from traditional accounts.

## Optimal Contribution Strategy

1. Contribute to 401(k) up to the employer match (free money first)
2. Max out a Roth IRA if eligible ($7,000)
3. Return to 401(k) and contribute up to the annual maximum ($23,000)
4. If still saving more, open a taxable brokerage account
