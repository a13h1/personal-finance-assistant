# Capital Gains Tax

Capital gains tax is levied on the profit earned when you sell an investment for more than you paid for it. Understanding how capital gains work is essential for tax-efficient investing, as the timing and type of your investment sales can dramatically affect your after-tax returns.

## What Is a Capital Gain?

A capital gain occurs when you sell a capital asset—stocks, bonds, real estate, mutual funds—for more than your cost basis (what you paid for it, including commissions).

**Capital Gain = Sale Price - Cost Basis**

If you bought 100 shares of a stock at $50 each ($5,000 total) and sold at $80 each ($8,000), your capital gain is $3,000.

## Short-Term vs. Long-Term Capital Gains

**Short-term capital gains** (assets held 1 year or less) are taxed as ordinary income at your regular marginal tax rate (up to 37%).

**Long-term capital gains** (assets held more than 1 year) are taxed at preferential rates:
- **0%:** Income up to $47,025 (single) / $94,050 (married, 2024)
- **15%:** Income from $47,026 to $518,900 (single)
- **20%:** Income over $518,900 (single)

**Example of the difference:**
$10,000 gain for someone in the 24% ordinary income bracket:
- Short-term: Pay $2,400 in federal taxes
- Long-term: Pay $1,500 in federal taxes (15% rate)
- **Savings from holding 1 year+: $900**

## Cost Basis Methods

Your taxable gain depends on which shares you designate as "sold" when you own shares purchased at different prices:

**FIFO (First In, First Out):** Default method; oldest shares sold first. Can trigger larger gains if early shares were purchased at lower prices.

**LIFO (Last In, First Out):** Most recently purchased shares sold first. Not permitted by the IRS for mutual funds.

**Specific Identification:** Choose exactly which shares to sell. Most flexible; allows you to minimize taxes by selecting shares with the highest cost basis.

**Average Cost Basis:** Averages all purchase prices. Only permitted for mutual funds and ETFs; simple but not always optimal.

## Capital Losses and Tax-Loss Harvesting

A capital loss occurs when you sell an investment for less than your cost basis. Capital losses can be used to offset capital gains:

**Netting rules:**
1. Net short-term gains against short-term losses
2. Net long-term gains against long-term losses
3. If net short-term result and net long-term result have opposite signs, combine them

**Annual deduction limit:**
If total losses exceed total gains, you can deduct up to $3,000 from ordinary income per year. Remaining losses carry forward to future years indefinitely.

**Tax-loss harvesting in practice:**
Systematically selling investments at a loss in late December to offset gains realized earlier in the year. The proceeds are immediately reinvested in a similar (but not identical) investment to maintain market exposure.

**Wash Sale Rule:** The IRS disallows a loss if you purchase the same or "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale. You can sell an S&P 500 ETF (SPY) and immediately buy a different S&P 500 ETF (IVV) without triggering the wash sale rule.

## Special Capital Gains Situations

**Inherited Assets (Stepped-Up Basis):**
Assets inherited receive a "step-up" in cost basis to the fair market value at the date of death. If your parent bought stock for $10,000 that's worth $100,000 when they die and you inherit it, your cost basis is $100,000—not $10,000. Any future appreciation from $100,000 is your taxable gain.

**Gifted Assets:**
Recipient receives the donor's original cost basis. If you give appreciated stock, the recipient will owe capital gains on the full appreciation when they sell.

**Real Estate:**
Primary residence exclusion: Can exclude up to $250,000 (single) or $500,000 (married) of gain on the sale of your primary home if you've lived in it for 2 of the last 5 years.

## Estimated Taxes on Capital Gains

If you sell investments with large capital gains mid-year, you may owe estimated taxes quarterly to avoid an underpayment penalty. The penalty applies if you owe more than $1,000 in taxes and haven't paid enough through withholding or estimated payments.
