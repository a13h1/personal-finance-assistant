# Diversification

Diversification is the practice of spreading investments across different assets, sectors, geographies, and asset classes to reduce risk. It is one of the most fundamental principles of sound investing, often summarized as "don't put all your eggs in one basket."

## Why Diversification Works

Different investments respond differently to economic conditions. When technology stocks decline, healthcare stocks might hold steady. When U.S. markets struggle, international markets may outperform. When stocks fall sharply, bonds often rise as investors seek safety.

By holding a variety of uncorrelated or negatively correlated assets, you reduce the impact that any single investment's poor performance can have on your overall portfolio.

## The Math of Diversification

Consider investing all $10,000 in one stock. If that company goes bankrupt, you lose everything. Now consider spreading $10,000 across 10 stocks equally. If one goes bankrupt, you lose at most 10% of your portfolio—devastating, but not catastrophic.

Research shows that most of the benefits of diversification are achieved with 20-30 individual stocks across different sectors. Beyond that point, adding more stocks produces diminishing diversification benefits.

## Types of Diversification

**Asset Class Diversification:**
- Stocks (equities) for growth
- Bonds (fixed income) for stability
- Real estate for income and inflation protection
- Cash for liquidity

**Geographic Diversification:**
- Domestic stocks (U.S.)
- International developed markets (Europe, Japan, Australia)
- Emerging markets (China, India, Brazil)

**Sector Diversification:**
The 11 GICS sectors should be represented proportionally:
- Technology
- Healthcare
- Financials
- Consumer Discretionary
- Consumer Staples
- Industrials
- Energy
- Utilities
- Materials
- Real Estate
- Communication Services

**Market Cap Diversification:**
- Large-cap: Stable, established companies
- Mid-cap: Balanced growth and stability
- Small-cap: Higher growth potential, more volatility

## Correlation and Modern Portfolio Theory

Nobel laureate Harry Markowitz developed Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), which demonstrates mathematically that a portfolio of assets with low correlation to each other produces a better risk-adjusted return than any single asset.

The key insight: you can reduce portfolio risk without necessarily reducing expected returns, simply by combining assets that don't move in lockstep.

## Common Diversification Mistakes

**Over-diversification (deworsification):** Owning too many funds with overlapping holdings achieves little additional risk reduction while increasing complexity and cost.

**Sector concentration without realizing it:** Owning Apple, Microsoft, Google, and Amazon seems diversified, but all are large-cap U.S. technology companies.

**Home country bias:** Many investors overweight their domestic market. International stocks represent roughly 40% of global market capitalization—ignoring them concentrates risk.

**False diversification in bonds:** During the 2022 bear market, both stocks and bonds declined simultaneously—a reminder that diversification doesn't eliminate all risk.

## Simple Diversification with ETFs

A globally diversified portfolio can be achieved with 3 ETFs:
1. U.S. Total Stock Market (VTI)
2. International Total Stock Market (VXUS)
3. U.S. Bond Market (BND)

Allocations might be 60% VTI / 20% VXUS / 20% BND for a moderate investor.
