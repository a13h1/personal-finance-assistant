# Budgeting Basics

A budget is a plan for how you will spend and save your money each month. It is the cornerstone of financial health—without knowing where your money goes, it's impossible to make intentional choices about saving and investing. Budgeting is not about restriction; it's about directing money toward what matters most to you.

## Why Budget?

Most people dramatically underestimate their spending in categories like dining out, entertainment, and subscriptions. Budgeting creates awareness and intentionality:

- **Avoid overspending:** Know exactly what you can afford
- **Build savings:** Allocate money to savings before spending on discretionary items
- **Reduce financial stress:** Knowing you have a plan reduces anxiety
- **Achieve goals faster:** Direct money toward priorities instead of unconscious spending
- **Prepare for irregular expenses:** Budget for annual expenses (car registration, holiday gifts) monthly

## Common Budgeting Methods

### The 50/30/20 Rule

A simple, popular framework dividing after-tax income into three categories:

- **50% Needs:** Housing, food, utilities, transportation, insurance, minimum debt payments
- **30% Wants:** Dining out, entertainment, subscriptions, hobbies, travel
- **20% Savings and debt payoff:** Emergency fund, retirement, investments, extra debt payments

**Example on $5,000/month take-home:**
- Needs: $2,500
- Wants: $1,500
- Savings/debt: $1,000

The 50/30/20 rule works well as a starting framework. High-cost-of-living areas may require adjusting (e.g., 60/20/20).

### Zero-Based Budgeting

Every dollar of income is assigned a purpose: spending, saving, or investing. The goal is for income minus all allocations to equal zero.

This method requires more tracking but provides maximum control and often reveals the most savings opportunities.

### Pay Yourself First

Automatically transfer savings to investment accounts on payday before budgeting the rest. This reverses the typical approach (spend first, save what's left) and ensures saving happens consistently.

**Implementation:**
1. Set up automatic transfer to HYSA (emergency fund) and IRA on payday
2. Budget the remaining income for expenses

### Envelope Method

Allocate cash into envelopes for different spending categories. When an envelope is empty, you're done spending in that category. Works exceptionally well for people who overspend on discretionary items.

Digital equivalent: Apps like YNAB (You Need a Budget) replicate this system digitally.

## Essential Budget Categories

**Fixed Expenses (same every month):**
- Rent/mortgage payment
- Car payment
- Insurance premiums (health, auto, renters/homeowners)
- Loan payments
- Streaming subscriptions

**Variable Needs (amount fluctuates but still necessary):**
- Groceries
- Utilities (electric, gas, water)
- Gas/transportation
- Healthcare/medical

**Discretionary (wants—adjustable):**
- Dining out and restaurants
- Entertainment (concerts, events, movies)
- Shopping (clothing, home goods)
- Hobbies
- Travel and vacations

**Irregular Expenses (annual or occasional—budget monthly):**
- Car maintenance and registration
- Holiday gifts
- Annual subscriptions
- Home maintenance
- Property taxes (if not escrowed)

## Building Your First Budget

1. **Calculate monthly take-home income** (after taxes and deductions)
2. **Track spending for 30 days** to understand current habits (use bank/credit card statements)
3. **List all fixed expenses** with exact amounts
4. **Estimate variable expenses** based on recent history
5. **Calculate available for savings:** Income minus all expenses
6. **Identify cuts:** Find discretionary spending to reduce and redirect to savings

## Budgeting Tools

- **Spreadsheets:** Google Sheets and Excel; free and fully customizable
- **YNAB:** Most rigorous zero-based budgeting; $14.99/month; strong ROI for people serious about budgeting
- **Mint/Copilot:** Automatic transaction import and categorization
- **Your bank's app:** Many banks offer built-in spending categorization tools

## Common Budgeting Mistakes

1. **Forgetting irregular expenses:** Car repairs, medical bills, annual fees
2. **Being too restrictive:** Budgets with no "fun money" typically fail
3. **Not tracking actual spending:** A budget is only useful if you compare plan to reality
4. **Giving up after one bad month:** Treat overruns as learning opportunities, not failures
5. **Using gross income instead of net:** Budget with take-home pay
