# Bonds Basics

Bonds are fixed-income securities that represent loans made by investors to borrowers—typically governments or corporations. When you buy a bond, you are lending money to the issuer in exchange for periodic interest payments and the return of the principal when the bond matures.

## Key Bond Terminology

**Face Value (Par Value):** The amount the bond is worth at maturity, typically $1,000 per bond.

**Coupon Rate:** The annual interest rate paid on the bond's face value. A bond with a $1,000 face value and 5% coupon pays $50 per year in interest.

**Maturity Date:** The date when the bond expires and the issuer repays the principal.

**Yield to Maturity (YTM):** The total return anticipated if the bond is held until it matures, accounting for current price, coupon payments, and face value.

**Current Yield:** Annual coupon payment divided by current market price.

## Types of Bonds

**U.S. Treasury Bonds:**
Issued by the U.S. government; considered the safest bonds available. Types include:
- Treasury Bills (T-Bills): Mature in one year or less
- Treasury Notes: 2-10 year maturities
- Treasury Bonds: 20-30 year maturities
- TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities): Principal adjusts with inflation

**Municipal Bonds (Munis):**
Issued by states and local governments. Interest is usually exempt from federal taxes and often state taxes—especially valuable for high-income investors.

**Corporate Bonds:**
Issued by companies. Pay higher yields than Treasuries to compensate for higher default risk. Rated by agencies (Moody's, S&P) as investment grade (BBB/Baa or higher) or high-yield/junk (BB/Ba or lower).

**Agency Bonds:**
Issued by government-sponsored entities like Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Slightly higher yields than Treasuries with similar safety.

## How Bond Prices Work

Bond prices move inversely to interest rates. When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall; when rates fall, bond prices rise.

**Example:** You own a bond paying 3% interest. New bonds now pay 5%. Your 3% bond becomes less attractive, so its price drops until its yield is competitive with new bonds.

This interest rate sensitivity is measured by **duration**—longer-term bonds have higher duration and are more sensitive to rate changes.

## Credit Ratings

Bond credit ratings assess the likelihood of the issuer defaulting:
- **AAA/Aaa:** Highest quality (U.S. government, top corporations)
- **AA to BBB/Baa:** Investment grade; low to moderate default risk
- **BB/Ba and below:** Non-investment grade (junk bonds); higher risk, higher yield

## Role of Bonds in a Portfolio

Bonds serve several key functions:

1. **Stability:** Less volatile than stocks; buffer portfolio during equity downturns
2. **Income:** Regular coupon payments provide predictable cash flow
3. **Diversification:** Low or negative correlation with stocks in many environments
4. **Capital preservation:** Particularly important for retirees

The classic portfolio allocation rule allocates a percentage to bonds equal to the investor's age (e.g., a 60-year-old holds 60% bonds), though this has been updated given longer life expectancies.

## Bond Funds vs. Individual Bonds

**Individual bonds:** You receive exactly your principal back at maturity (if issuer doesn't default); requires larger capital for diversification; more complex to manage.

**Bond funds/ETFs:** Instant diversification; professional management; continuous pricing; but no guaranteed return of principal at a specific date.

For most individual investors, low-cost bond ETFs (like BND or AGG) provide the best balance of diversification, cost, and simplicity.

## Current Yield Environment

Bond yields rise during periods of high inflation and economic growth; they fall during recessions. The Federal Reserve's federal funds rate significantly influences short-term bond yields.
